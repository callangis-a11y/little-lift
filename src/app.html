For the people in your life

Short, practical guides for the situations people ask about most.

Spot · Speak · Support

Worried about someone?

You don't need to be an expert. Noticing, asking and staying with them can make a real difference.

Send a check-in text

Sometimes the hardest part is the first message. Pick one, make it yours, and send it.

Changes you might notice

  • Pulling away from friends, family or things they used to enjoy
  • Sleeping much more or much less, or always seeming tired
  • More irritable, anxious or flat than usual
  • Drinking or using more to cope
  • Falling behind at work or school
  • Talking about feeling trapped, hopeless or like a burden
  • Talking or posting about death, suicide or not being around
  • Giving things away, saying goodbye or sorting out their affairs
  • Seeming suddenly calm after a very low time
  • A big loss or setback, like a break-up, job loss or death

Notice it in yourself, too

The same signs apply to you. If you recognise several, try a check-in on the Today tab or talk to someone you trust.

The last few signs mean act today. If someone talks about ending their life, take it seriously. If they're in immediate danger, call 000 and stay with them.

Call 000

Starting the conversation

  1. Pick your moment.

    Somewhere private, with time to talk. Side by side, on a walk or in the car, can feel easier than face to face.

  2. Ask directly.

    Mention what you've noticed, then ask how they're going. Asking won't make things worse.

  3. Listen more than you talk.

    Don't rush to fix it. Let silences happen and take what they say seriously.

  4. Check back in.

    A text a few days later shows you meant it.

Things you could say

If they say yes, they're thinking about suicide

  • Stay calm. Thank them for telling you
  • Ask if they've thought about how or when. It helps you know how urgent it is
  • If they have a plan, or you think they're at risk right now, don't leave them alone. Call 000
  • If it's safe to, help put away anything they could use to hurt themselves. Don't put yourself in danger
  • Don't promise to keep it a secret. Their safety comes first
  • Call Lifeline (13 11 14) or Suicide Call Back Service (1300 659 467) together
  • Afterwards, look after yourself. You can call these lines for yourself too

Helping them take the next step

  • Ask what would help, rather than assuming
  • Suggest seeing a GP. They can talk it through and refer to Medicare-subsidised psychology if it would help
  • Offer to sit with them while they call a support line or book an appointment
  • Look after yourself too. Supporting someone is heavy

Living with ADHD

Brains that work differently

ADHD affects attention, impulses and activity levels, in kids and adults. What used to be called ADD is now the "inattentive" type of ADHD. With the right support, people with ADHD can thrive.

Who is this about?

Focus Sprint

Starting is often the hardest part. Pick one task and a short sprint. When a stray thought pops up, park it in the brain dump and keep going.

10:00

Things that help

  • Get it out of your head: calendars, phone alarms, a whiteboard by the door
  • Shrink tasks until the first step feels almost too easy
  • Work alongside someone, in person or on a call ("body doubling")
  • Keep keys, wallet and meds in one home spot
  • Move your body and protect your sleep. Both affect focus
  • Go easy on yourself. ADHD isn't laziness or a character flaw

Weekly check-in

Ten minutes, once a week, side by side. It stops small things building into big ones. Take turns answering.

If your partner has ADHD

  • Separate the person from the symptom. Forgetting isn't the same as not caring
  • Nagging rarely works. Agree on reminders and systems together
  • Notice and name the effort, not just the misses
  • Avoid slipping into a parent role. You're a team
  • Look after your own wellbeing and support

If you're the one with ADHD

  • Use shared calendars and timers so your partner isn't your memory
  • When things get heated, take a break and come back to it
  • Repeat back what you heard to make sure you've got it
  • Split jobs by strengths, in a way that works for you both

Routine builder

Kids with ADHD do best with the same steps in the same order. Tick them off together. Ticks reset each day.

Tips for parents

  • Give one instruction at a time, up close, and ask them to say it back
  • Catch them being good. Praise quickly and specifically
  • Use visual charts and timers for routines and screen time
  • Plenty of movement and a steady bedtime help everyone
  • Talk to their teacher about what works at home
  • Look after yourself. Parenting a child with ADHD is a lot
Could it be ADHD?

Inattentive signs: easily distracted, loses things, struggles to start or finish tasks, forgetful, seems not to listen.

Hyperactive or impulsive signs: restless, fidgety, talks a lot, interrupts, acts before thinking.

Everyone does these sometimes. With ADHD they're frequent, started in childhood, last more than 6 months, and show up in more than one place, like home and school or work.

Getting assessed in Australia: start with your GP. They can refer you to a paediatrician (for children), or a psychiatrist or psychologist. Adults may be asked for school reports, as signs usually start in childhood.

Treatment can include strategies and coaching, therapy such as CBT, parenting programs, and medication. A psychologist can assess, but only a doctor can prescribe medication. Your GP can talk through what suits you.

Sleep better

Good sleep, better days

Sleep and mood feed each other. A rough night makes everything harder, and stress makes sleep harder. Small, steady habits help both.

Habits that help

  • Get up at the same time every day, even on weekends. It sets your body clock
  • Get daylight in the morning, and keep evenings dim
  • Give yourself 30 to 60 minutes to wind down before bed
  • Keep caffeine to the morning, and go easy on alcohol. It breaks up sleep later in the night
  • Keep your bed for sleep. If you've been awake for about 20 minutes, get up, do something calm in dim light, and go back when you feel sleepy
  • Busy mind at night? Write worries down and give them a set time tomorrow
  • Try not to check the clock during the night
  • If you nap, keep it short (under 30 minutes) and before mid-afternoon

When to see your GP

If poor sleep has gone on for more than a few weeks, you snore loudly or stop breathing in your sleep, or sleep problems come with low mood, or you feel very sleepy during the day (especially when driving), talk to your GP. Ask about CBT for insomnia, which is the recommended first treatment for ongoing sleep problems.

After a suicide loss

There's no right way to grieve

Losing someone to suicide can bring shock, sadness, anger, guilt and questions that may never have answers. All of it is normal.

Things that can help

  • It's not your fault. People who die by suicide are usually in pain that others can't see or fix
  • Talk about the person and how you feel, with people you trust
  • Keep up the basics: eating, sleeping, moving, even a little
  • Expect hard days around anniversaries and birthdays
  • Join a support group with others who understand. StandBy can help you find one
  • Children grieve too. Use simple, honest words. StandBy can help you talk with them
  • See your GP, and let them know what's happened

When to get more help

If you feel hopeless, can't function day to day, or have thoughts of suicide yourself, reach out now. You don't have to carry this alone.

Supporting someone who's grieving

  • Say the person's name. Silence can feel like they've been forgotten
  • Listen without trying to explain or fix it
  • Offer practical help, like meals, lifts or school pick-ups
  • Keep checking in after the funeral, when support often drops away

Move for your mind

Move your body, lift your mood

A large 2023 University of South Australia review, drawing on more than 1,000 trials, found regular exercise helped reduce symptoms of depression and anxiety. For some people the effect was similar to other treatments. Every bit counts, and you don't need a gym to start.

Exercise works best alongside other care. Keep any current treatment going and talk to your GP before changing it.

Guided sessions

Go at your own pace. Swap any move for marching on the spot. Stop if anything hurts, and check with your GP if you have a health condition.

Making it stick

  • Pick something you enjoy. You'll keep doing it
  • Train with a mate. It's exercise and a catch-up in one
  • Walking, jogging, yoga and strength training have some of the best evidence for mood
  • Short and regular beats long and rare
  • The Australian guidelines for adults are 150–300 minutes of moderate activity a week, plus strength work twice a week. Under 18? Aim for about an hour a day

About Little Lift

Little Lift is a free wellbeing app for everyone, proudly supported by the Mental Awareness Foundation. Small steps, any time.

Your privacy

  • No account, no sign-up, no ads.
  • Your check-ins, notes, safety plan and journal stay on this device. They're never sent to us or anyone else.
  • If you share this phone, anyone using it could see them. You can delete them any time.
  • The app's fonts load from Google, which sees your device's internet address, as with any website visit. Nothing you write is sent.

Your data

Everything you've saved lives only in this browser. Save a backup so you don't lose it if you clear your browser or change phones. The file holds your private entries, including notes, worries and your safety plan, in plain text. Keep it somewhere safe.
